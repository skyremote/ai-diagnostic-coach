# What the client actually receives

A diagnostic's deliverables should make the client feel like they got something they
could not have produced internally — evidence, a defensible ranking, and a concrete
next step — not a generic template with their logo pasted on the cover.

## The written report

Structure it to be skimmed by an executive and defended by a specialist. Both readers
exist in the same room; the report has to survive both.

**Lead with the bottom line, not the process.** The first page should state the
headline finding and the top recommendation in plain language, before any methodology,
before any "about us." A reader who only reads page one should still walk away knowing
what to do next. This is the single most common failure mode in diagnostic reports:
burying the answer under forty pages of how you got there.

**State your confidence, not just your conclusion.** Where a finding is backed by
direct evidence across many interviews, say so plainly. Where it rests on a smaller
sample or an inference, say that too. A report that presents every claim with the same
flat authority is less trustworthy than one that's honest about which findings are
solid and which are directional — and a sharp reader will notice the difference
immediately if you fake certainty you don't have.

**Show the evidence trail, not just the conclusion.** For each priority opportunity:
what specifically was observed (which role, roughly how often, roughly what it costs
in time or money), what the recommendation is, what it would take to build, what could
go wrong, and how it was scored against the other candidates. A reader should be able
to trace every recommendation back to something that was actually said or observed —
if they can't, the report reads as opinion with a consultant's letterhead on it.

**Structure of the core sections:**

1. **Executive summary** — the headline finding and top recommendation, one page.
2. **Current-state findings** — what the interviews and data actually showed, organised
   by role or function, not by AI-use-case. This section earns its credibility by
   sounding like it came from the client's own business, not a generic template.
3. **Opportunity inventory** — the full longlist from stage 3 of the method, so the
   client can see the breadth of what was considered, not just the narrow shortlist
   that survived.
4. **Prioritised roadmap** — the scored, sequenced set from stage 4, organised into
   quick wins / foundational work / strategic bets, each with its scoring rationale.
5. **Risks and dependencies** — what has to be true for this roadmap to work: data
   access, budget, a system nobody's allowed to touch, organisational appetite for
   change. Naming these up front is what makes the roadmap credible rather than
   aspirational.
6. **Recommended next step** — the smallest credible first move, its cost, its
   success measure, and who should own it. This is the sentence the whole report was
   building toward; don't let it get lost in an appendix.

**Format matters less than specificity.** A concise document with sharp, evidenced
findings beats a long one every time. If the report needs a spreadsheet (a scored
opportunity matrix) or a diagram (a workflow map, a phased timeline) to make a finding
land, include it as a companion artefact rather than trying to force it into prose.

## The live presentation

The written report and the room presentation are different deliverables serving
different purposes, and conflating them is a common mistake. The report is read
alone, later, and needs to stand on its own. The presentation is live, time-boxed, and
exists to get a decision.

- **Six or seven slides, not a slide-by-slide narration of the report.** Every slide
  should earn its place by moving the room toward the decision, not by proving you did
  the work — the report already proves that.
  Lead with the bottom line. Save methodology for one slide, not five.
- **Bring the room a live object to react to, not just a projected outcome.** Where
  feasible, a rough working demo, an interactive mock, or a before/after on a real
  (anonymised) sample of the client's own data does more for a room's confidence than
  another slide of bullet points. Keep it visibly rough — a proof of concept that looks
  finished invites "why isn't this shipped yet" instead of "yes, build this."
- **End on the ask, explicitly.** State the recommended next step, who you need it
  from, and by when, out loud, in the room. A roadmap presentation that ends on
  "any questions?" without a stated next action has quietly turned a decision meeting
  back into an information meeting.

## What "done" looks like from the client's side

After a well-run diagnostic, the client should be able to answer, without you in the
room:

- What did you actually find, in your own operation, that you didn't already know?
- What's the first thing you'd do, and roughly what would it cost and take?
- Why that one first, and not a different one on the list?
- What has to be true for the rest of the roadmap to work?

If they can't answer those from the deliverables alone, the diagnostic under-delivered
regardless of how much work went into producing it.
