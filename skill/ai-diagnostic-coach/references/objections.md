# Objections and how to handle them

Real objections a diagnostic engagement runs into, and how to work through each one
without either caving on scope/price or getting defensive. Use these for rehearsal —
see the `roleplay` verb in [../SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) — and for live coaching when a
real deal hits one of these.

## "Just tell us what tool to buy"

The buyer wants a shortcut past the diagnosis straight to a purchase decision, usually
because someone senior has already half-decided the answer.

**What's actually happening:** either they've been burned by a slow consultant before
and are pattern-matching you to that experience, or they genuinely believe the
bottleneck is picking the right vendor rather than understanding the workflow.

**How to handle it:** don't argue the abstract case for diagnosis. Ask one concrete
question: "which three workflows would this tool actually touch, and how much time do
they currently cost?" If they can answer specifically, they may not need a full
diagnostic — say so; forcing a five-stage process onto a problem that's already
well-understood is how diagnostics earn a reputation as padding. If they can't answer
it, that gap *is* the diagnostic, and you now have a concrete example to point at
rather than a philosophical argument about methodology.

## "This is expensive for something that isn't the actual build"

The buyer is comparing the diagnostic fee to what they imagine implementation would
cost, and finding the ratio uncomfortable.

**What's actually happening:** the value of *not* building the wrong thing hasn't
been made concrete yet. Nobody feels the cost of an avoided mistake the way they feel
an invoice.

**How to handle it:** don't defend the fee in isolation. Reframe against the
counterfactual: what does a wrong six-month build cost, in money and in the internal
credibility of "AI" as a project category after it fails? A diagnostic that's 10-20%
of a likely implementation budget and materially improves the odds that budget is
spent well is cheap insurance, not an add-on cost. If you have a real (anonymised)
example of a diagnostic that changed the recommended build and saved a client from a
bad bet, this is where it earns its keep.

## "We already know what we need, we just want you to build it"

The buyer is trying to skip straight to implementation with an unvalidated scope.

**What's actually happening:** sometimes they're right and a diagnostic would be
theatre. More often, "we know what we need" means one senior person's opinion has
become the plan by default, and nobody has checked it against how the work actually
happens on the ground.

**How to handle it:** ask what evidence the current scope is based on. "Talked to a
few people" is not the same as structured interviews across the roles that would
actually use the thing. If there's real evidence already, respect it and scope
accordingly — possibly a compressed diagnostic that validates rather than discovers.
If there isn't, say plainly that you'll build whatever they want, but you won't
warrant the outcome of a build whose scope nobody has actually tested — and that's
what the diagnostic buys them: a scope you're willing to stand behind.

## "Can you do this for free, we'll pay you back in the implementation work"

The buyer wants to eliminate their own risk by shifting it onto you.

**What's actually happening:** either genuine budget constraint, or a test of how
much you believe in your own product. Both are legitimate to probe, but they're
different conversations.

**How to handle it:** separate the two. If it's budget, a smaller, faster, cheaper
diagnostic scoped to a single team is a real answer — better than free, because a
paid (even small) engagement is one the client takes seriously enough to give you real
access. If it's a test, hold the line: a free diagnostic that gets treated as
disposable produces disposable engagement from stakeholders, which produces a weak
diagnosis, which produces a recommendation nobody trusts enough to act on. The fee
isn't just revenue — it's what makes the interviewees show up prepared.

## "We don't have budget authority in the room, let us take it upstairs"

The person you're pricing to can't actually approve the number.

**What's actually happening:** this is sometimes true and sometimes a soft no dressed
up as a process step.

**How to handle it:** ask directly who does have authority and whether you can be in
the room, or on the follow-up call, when the number is presented upstairs. A genuine
process person will welcome this — they want you to help them sell it internally. A
soft no will resist it. Either way you've learned something real, and you haven't
moved your price to find out.

## "Your diagnostic sounds like every other AI audit pitch"

The buyer has seen generic "AI readiness assessment" positioning before, usually
attached to a thinly disguised sales funnel, and is pre-emptively discounting yours.

**What's actually happening:** the category is genuinely crowded with exactly that —
low-price audits that exist to generate implementation leads, delivered by people with
no engineering credibility behind the recommendation.

**How to handle it:** don't argue you're different in the abstract — show it. Lead
with what makes the recommendation trustworthy: that you (or your team) actually build
production systems, so the feasibility scoring in stage 4 isn't a guess; that the
diagnostic produces a decision the client can act on with anyone, not just you; that
the report names trade-offs and risks, not just upside. Concrete process detail beats
a claim of differentiation every time.

## "What if you recommend something and it doesn't work"

The buyer is probing what happens if the roadmap turns out to be wrong.

**What's actually happening:** a reasonable question about how confident you actually
are, dressed as a hypothetical about failure.

**How to handle it:** don't oversell certainty you don't have — that's the fastest way
to lose credibility the moment reality diverges from the roadmap. Be explicit that
the scoring model (see [method.md](method.md)) exists precisely because feasibility
and risk are assessed up front, and that the "quick win first" sequencing is designed
to surface a wrong call within weeks, not after a six-month build. The honest answer
is: the diagnostic doesn't guarantee the recommendation is right, it front-loads the
cheapest possible way to find out.

## General posture across all of these

Don't manufacture confidence you don't have, and don't concede scope or price just to
keep a conversation friendly. The diagnostic's entire value proposition rests on it
being a rigorous, evidence-based process — every time you skip a step or shave the fee
to close faster, you're quietly undermining the thing the client is actually paying
for.
